About

I came to product from the least glamorous end of software.

No growth team, no startup, no design school. Firmware, in defense. It turns out to be unusually good preparation.

I write embedded software for electronic warfare systems in Southern California. It is not the kind of work you demo at a party. There is no interface. The best possible outcome is that an operator, in a situation where being wrong is expensive, never once has to think about the thing I built.

That constraint turned out to be the most useful product training I could have had.

What the work actually teaches

Embedded systems are the discipline of scarcity. You get a fixed budget of memory, cycles, and power, and a hard deadline measured in microseconds. You cannot buy your way out with more infrastructure. Every feature you add has a cost you can measure and someone will make you justify.

That is the same negotiation a product manager runs, just with different units. Roadmap capacity instead of clock cycles. Cognitive load instead of memory. The instinct is identical: what does this cost, what does it displace, and is the user better off?

The second thing it teaches is failure behavior. In real-time systems you spend more time on what happens when something breaks than on the happy path — because the happy path is the easy part and the degraded path is where people get hurt. Most consumer products get this backwards. They are beautiful when everything works and useless the moment a ride goes down, a network drops, or a battery dies. Those are the moments that determine whether someone trusts your product, and they are almost always under-designed.

Why I'm moving

I like the systems work. What I kept noticing is that the decisions I found most interesting were happening upstream of me — which problem we picked, who we picked it for, what we were willing to trade away. I have spent the last stretch of my career pushing toward those conversations: writing internal proposals, socializing them with chief engineers and program leadership, and learning that the technical argument is rarely the one that decides anything.

That work is product work. It is just not called that where I do it.

What I'm looking for

Products where a lot of people use a shared, constrained system at the same time and the experience either holds up or falls apart in public. Theme parks and live venues are the clearest version of this — physical capacity, real-time demand, and a guest who paid a lot of money and will remember exactly how the day felt. Consumer platforms and technical products with genuinely hard systems underneath are the same shape of problem.

If that's what you're building, the case studies are the fastest way to see how I think.
